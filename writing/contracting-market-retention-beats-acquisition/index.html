In a contracting car market, share moves to whoever loses the fewest customers

Toyota's volume in Saudi Arabia fell 17.4% in the first half of 2026 and its share rose from 26.7% to 28.8%. Losing less was the growth strategy.

Published 2026-08-21 · Alawi AlAlawi, Digitalization Manager, BMW Group KSA, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia

The numbers behind this

  • Toyota's KSA volume fell 17.4% in H1 2026 while its market share rose from 26.7% to 28.8%.
  • Published H1 2026 totals for the Saudi market conflict sharply between sources, with no methodology disclosed by either. Brand-level share movements are the more defensible read.

Toyota's volume in Saudi Arabia fell 17.4% in the first half of 2026. Over the same period its market share rose from 26.7% to 28.8%.

Both numbers are true at once, and the gap between them is the whole point. In a contracting market, share is not won by selling more. It is won by losing fewer customers than the people next to you.

That is a different operational problem from the one most regional digital programs were built to solve.

What most programs were built for

Nearly every automotive digital investment I have seen in this region over the past decade was an acquisition build. More leads. Faster lead response. Another channel. Better attribution on media spend. A chat widget, then a WhatsApp channel, then a social inbox.

That was the right build. The market was growing, and in a growing market the marginal customer is a new customer, so the marginal system should be a lead system.

When the market contracts, the marginal customer is one you already have. The systems that find them are different systems, and in most importers they are the ones nobody instrumented.

Where the retention signals actually live

They are not in the campaign dashboard. They are in the dealer management system, in records that were never joined to anything that reports upward:

  • Service interval drift. The customer who used to come at ten months and now comes at fourteen. Nothing has gone wrong yet. It is about to.
  • The second workshop visit that never happened. First service completed under warranty, then nothing. This is the single clearest defection signal in automotive and it is usually invisible because it is an absence, and absences do not generate rows.
  • Replacement cycle stretch. A parc that used to turn over at four years turning over at six. That shows up as a demand collapse two years late if nobody is watching the distribution.
  • Declined work not followed up. A quoted repair the customer deferred, with no mechanism to return to it.

None of these require new data collection. All of them are already in the DMS. What they require is a query, an owner and a place to report.

Why this is a data problem before it is a CRM problem

The reason these signals stay dark is rarely that nobody thought of them. It is that the customer record is not stable enough to compute them.

To measure interval drift you need to know that the person who came in March and the person who came in September are the same person. In a database with duplicate records — one from the sales transaction, one created at the service desk, one from a campaign import — they are three people with one visit each, and the drift is unmeasurable. The signal exists in the raw data and is destroyed by the record structure.

That is why retention work in automotive tends to start somewhere unglamorous: de-duplication, mandatory fields, edit controls, audit logging. It looks like housekeeping. It is the precondition for every retention metric downstream.

The strongest argument against this

If the contraction is driven by affordability rather than by satisfaction, retention work does not save a customer who cannot buy. A household priced out of a replacement purchase is not a churn problem; it is a financing problem, and no amount of service-interval instrumentation will bring them back. On that reading, the budget is better spent defending price position and building financing options.

What would change my mind

Evidence that the Saudi contraction is credit-driven at the household level rather than confidence-driven. Loan rejection rates, average tenor, down-payment sizes — if those moved first, the answer moves from retention to financing, and I would rather know that before spending a year instrumenting the workshop.

One caveat on the market data itself: published totals for the Saudi market in H1 2026 conflict sharply between credible sources, in opposite directions, with no methodology disclosed by either. I would not build a case on any market total right now. Brand-level share movements, which both sources broadly agree on, are the more defensible ground — and they say the same thing.
